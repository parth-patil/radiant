A short history of mind mapping

Branching diagrams of ideas are ancient, but the colourful, radial, picture-led "Mind Map" as a named technique belongs to Tony Buzan, who popularised it from the 1970s and built a business around it. This page separates what the sources establish from what is legend, follows the technique into software, and ends with one consolidated timeline.

Evidence

Buzan himself rejects Porphyry, Leonardo da Vinci and Isaac Newton as inventors of the mind map, and a published critic calls the claim that he invented the technique "quite preposterous". Both positions are sourced below. The safest statement is that Buzan named, standardised and commercialised a style of radial note-making, drawing on older practices of diagramming and doodling.

Before Buzan

Hierarchical diagrams of concepts are very old. Porphyry of Tyros, in the third century, graphically visualised Aristotle's categories as a branching diagram, among the earliest diagrams of this kind. The Catalan thinker Ramon Llull (1235 to 1315) used similar techniques. Wikipedia These are trees: they have a root and branches, but they are drawn as structured logic diagrams rather than as free, picture-rich notes.

Buzan's own view of these ancestors is firm. In Mind Map Mastery (Watkins, 2018, ch. 3) he writes that Porphyry's tree has "no central image" and that "The thinking here is not radiant". He says Leonardo "did not use colour schematically in his diagrams" and that Newton's diagrams "were monochromatic" and resembled a "tree growing upward rather than expanding radiantly". He concludes: "To confuse centuries-old diagrams with modern Mind Maps is a little like mistaking a penny farthing bicycle for a supercharged motorcycle!" He does credit Darwin among those "who used diagrams to develop their ideas" and says Leonardo's notes combining words and images "helped shape the early stages of my research". In the book's preface he names Leonardo, Michelangelo, Curie, Einstein and Darwin as parallels, whose "words and diagrams explode in all directions across the page", but not as inventors of the technique. These quotations come from the book text read for this research (Mind Map Mastery, 2018, ch. 3 and preface); the copy was a PDF with no usable printed page numbers.

  1. 3rd century

    Porphyry's tree of categories

    Porphyry of Tyros graphically visualises Aristotle's categories. Buzan rejects it as a mind map because it has no central image. Wikipedia

  2. 1235 to 1315

    Ramon Llull

    Llull, who lived across these years, used similar diagramming techniques. Wikipedia

  3. c. July 1837

    Darwin's first evolutionary tree

    A notebook page with the words "I think" at the top is captioned as Darwin's first sketch of an evolutionary tree. The notes could not confirm which notebook it belongs to. Wikipedia

  4. 1950s and 1960s

    Buzan's "Brain Pattern Notes"

    By Buzan's own account in Mind Map Mastery (2018), his precursor was Brain Pattern Notes, at first called "Buzan Diagrams".

  5. 1969

    Semantic networks

    Collins and Quillian published "Retrieval Time From Semantic Memory" in the Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior 8. They found that semantic memory is organised like a library of interrelated categories or nodes. Buzan cites this for his account of memory (Mind Map Mastery, 2018, ch. 5 and reference list). The notes do not present it as a mind map precursor.

  6. 1970s

    Novak's concept maps

    Joseph D. Novak and his team at Cornell developed concept maps to represent students' emerging science knowledge, tied to constructivism and to Ausubel's 1968 Educational Psychology: A Cognitive View. Wikipedia The Irish Times dates the Cornell work to 1972. Irish Times 2015

  7. 1983

    Rico's clustering

    Gabriele Rico's Writing the Natural Way (1983) describes "clustering" around a "nucleus word" as a visual map of thoughts. This rests on a search summary only; none of the sources compare it to Buzan's technique. Palestra

Two further contextual facts come from the Irish Times: Edward de Bono coined "lateral thinking" in the 1960s, and Pelmanism was a large correspondence mind-training business in Britain in the early 1900s. Irish Times 2015

The Times obituary is cited by Wikipedia as saying Buzan's technique was inspired by Leonardo da Vinci, Albert Einstein and Novak's concept mapping. The Times text itself was not read, so this is a claim about inspiration reported at one remove. Serig (2011) is quoted as saying "the idea that he invented them is quite preposterous". Wikipedia

Tony Buzan

Anthony Peter "Tony" Buzan was born on 2 June 1942 in Palmers Green, Middlesex, and died on 13 April 2019, aged 76, at the John Radcliffe Hospital in Oxford, of a heart attack. His brother is the academic Barry Buzan. Wikipedia

He was educated at Kitsilano Secondary School in Vancouver and the University of British Columbia, where he studied psychology, English, mathematics and science. He spent 1965 to 1966 at Simon Fraser University as a charter student and first president of its student society, and was a Mensa member and editor of the International Journal of Mensa. Wikipedia

His own origin story, as told to the Irish Times in 2015, is that he noticed students who doodled on their notes got good exam results, was frustrated by how long note-taking took while he studied at UBC in the 1960s, and found that doodling had been used by geniuses such as Leonardo and Einstein. Irish Times 2015 In Mind Map Mastery he gives a second version: he underlined keywords in his notes and "realized they made up less than 10 percent of what I had written down. Yet these keywords unlocked core concepts" (Introduction). He dates the Mind Map to "the mid-1960s" in the preface and says "I introduced Mind Maps to the world in the 1960s" in the introduction, while Wikipedia dates the term to 1974.

Books and broadcasting

The BBC series Use Your Head (the 1970s; 1974 according to Wikipedia's article on mind maps) is where Wikipedia says the term "mind map" was introduced. Wikipedia It led to five books: Use Your Memory, Master Your Memory, Use Your Head, The Speed Reading Book and The Mind Map Book, which sold over 3 million by 2003. Wikipedia counts more than 80 books overall. Other titles it lists are Speed Reading (1971), Use Your Perfect Memory (1991) and Mind Map Mastery (2018). Wikipedia The Irish Times says 120 or more books in 30 languages, so the two sources disagree on the count. The dates of The Mind Map Book (1993, with Barry Buzan) and How to Mind Map (2002) were not confirmed from a source read here, and Barry Buzan's co-authorship role is not sourced.

Competitions

In Mind Map Mastery Buzan writes: "In 1991, Ray Keene and I co-founded the World Memory Championships, an event that is separate from the World Mind Mapping Championships ... ten different disciplines". The book's resources list gives the World Memory Sports Council as founded by Tony Buzan and Raymond Keene OBE in 1991, and the World Mind Mapping Council as founded by Phil Chambers and Tony Buzan. Wikipedia corroborates that the memory championship has taken place annually since 1991 except 1992, "originated by Tony Buzan and co founded by Tony Buzan and Ray Keene". Wikipedia WMC Wikipedia's article on Buzan adds that he co-founded the Mind Sports Olympiad and the Mind Body Spirit Festival and was involved in the World Championships of the Brain. Wikipedia

The World Mind Mapping Championships are marked on "20 different criteria, such as the impact of the imagery, the use of humour (for instance, through puns), the attractiveness of the Mind Map and its originality, and whether it obeys the fundamental principles of Mind Mapping". Tasks include mapping a lecture or a set text, and events have been held in the UK, Singapore, China and other countries. Liu Yan won gold at the 2016 championships (Mind Map Mastery, 2018, preface and resources). These criteria are a useful rubric for the quality of generated maps, taken up on the laws page.

The instructor business

The Irish Times (2015) reports books, DVDs, seminars and schools in Mumbai, Dubai, Singapore, Tokyo and Henley-on-Thames, and more than 700 ThinkBuzan licensed instructors accredited since 2010. Irish Times 2015 Phil Chambers, described as a World Mind Mapping Champion and a Buzan-trained instructor since 1995, wrote 101 Top Tips for Better Mind Maps, which targets hand-drawn maps. MMSB review Buzan's training organisation currently teaches "Five Laws of Mind Mapping" (Branches, Keywords, Colour, Pictures and Structure) and a licensed-instructor course, according to a search summary of its course pages that was not read in full. tonybuzan.com

Mind maps and concept maps

The two techniques are often confused, and Buzan spends a section of Mind Map Mastery separating them. The core difference is structure: a mind map has one central concept with branches radiating from it, while a concept map is a free-form network that may have several hubs and labels its links. Lanzing (1998) is quoted as saying "a mind map has only one main concept, while a concept map may have several". Wikipedia

The book's own contributor Marek Kasperski writes of making "very small concept maps to large ones" while following the Laws, which suggests the publisher uses the terms loosely. For the app, the useful point is that cross-links, which Buzan allows through arrows and codes, are the allowed exception to a strict single-centre tree. Wikipedia

Describing a product as a "Mind Map" may touch Buzan-related marks. Radiant should use "mind mapping" generically only after a registry check at the UK IPO, USPTO, DPMA and EUIPO. This is not legal advice.

From paper to software

The first software built with Buzan was iMindMap, made with Chris Griffiths. Buzan describes it in Mind Map Mastery (2018, ch. 6) as "over a decade since I teamed up with Chris Griffiths to create the first effective digital Mind Mapping software: iMindMap", software "that reflects the brain's organic nature ... while obeying the Laws of Mind Mapping", and says it was "used by over one million people". In an e-mail interview with Chuck Frey (undated on the page) he said the aim was a "perfect marriage between the computer and the infinitely associative organic nature of natural human thought", called iMindMap "the first software in history to accomplish this goal", and said the team would "go down in history alongside Gutenberg". Criticism

Critics make four main points. The first is scientific: cynics call brain lateralisation, on which Buzan relies, a scientific fallacy, and say the "10% of the brain" idea is wrong. Joshua Foer says Buzan has a "habit of lapsing into pseudoscience and hyperbole", and that a claim of thinking-tool use dropping from 98% in young children to under 15% in industry is where "credibility gives way to a sales pitch". Irish Times 2015 The article's statement that Buzan's ideas "rely on" the 10% claim is the journalist's and critics' framing, not verified from Buzan's text. In 2008 the same paper reported that his critics question "the way he invokes simplified scientific concepts", and that Buzan answers that detractors "need to look closely at the research before accusing him of peddling pseudoscience". Irish Times 2008

The second point is evidence: a reviewer says Buzan "rarely references any scientific support for his claims" and that the rules are "more like a supporting set of principles" than strict laws. Tornhill The third is the invention claim, covered above. The fourth is commercialisation, including the sales-brochure remark.

Practitioners also relax the rules. Jamie Nast wrote in 2009 that "Although there is some value in the laws when applied appropriately, some people feel their creativity is restricted by them", that clients nearly abandoned the tool "especially by the one-word-per-line law", and that "When and where the laws should be applied should be determined by the purpose for which the map is being created". She teaches the laws as guidelines. Nast 2009 Nancy Margulies's Mapping Inner Space is said by reader reviews to "break some traditional 'Mindmapping' rules", which she calls "Mindscaping"; the evidence is search summaries only, and publication dates conflict between 1991 and 2002. AbeBooks

Limits of this research

Not verified

The Guardian obituary could not be fetched, and the Times and BBC obituaries were not read. The Times claim about Buzan's inspiration is therefore second-hand.

  • Leonardo's notebooks as a precursor rest on Buzan's own claim, qualified in 2018. Darwin's notebook label ("Notebook B") is not confirmed.
  • No source was found for the origin of spider diagrams or for Evelyn Wood and speed reading as a precursor. Rico's clustering is known only from a search summary.
  • The 1991 date for the World Memory Championships is Buzan's own statement in 2018; Wikipedia corroborates the year but not the Sports Council's founding date.
  • First-hand wording was not found for Vanda North, Chris Griffiths, Michael Gelb, Joyce Wycoff or Nancy Margulies on relaxing the rules, and Phil Chambers's own site was not reached.
  • The iMindMap launch press material, the month of the Ayoa launch and the current trademark registry status remain unchecked. A critical article, "How iMindMap left ... lost", was in the search results but not read. Computing
  • No peer-reviewed rebuttal of "radiant thinking" was found.
