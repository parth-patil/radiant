What the evidence says about memorable maps

Research on learning and memory supports a handful of design choices for a mind-mapping app and gives no support to several widely repeated beliefs. The best-supported choices concern what the learner does with a map: building it, recalling it from memory and revisiting it on a spaced schedule. Pictures help when they carry meaning, and the evidence for decoration, curved lines and one-word branches is thin or absent.

Evidence

Four findings matter most for Radiant. First, building or reworking a map helps more than studying a finished one, which makes an AI that hands over a finished map a risk to learning. Second, retrieval practice and spaced review are the best-supported techniques in the whole literature, and mapping alone is not. Third, pictures help when they carry meaning and can slightly hurt when they are decoration. Fourth, distinctiveness works only by contrast, so a map in which every branch is equally ornate gains nothing from it.

Every claim below carries a strength badge: Strong for a meta-analysis or a result replicated many times, Moderate for several studies or one review, Weak for a single study, an abstract only or a conflicted result, Folklore where no support was found, and Contested where credible sources disagree. The research behind this page relied mostly on abstracts and search summaries because publishers blocked full texts. Each number is therefore labelled with where it came from, and the section "Limits of this research" lists what could not be obtained.

Do mind maps work?

Concept maps have good meta-analytic support, and the support is strongest when learners construct the map themselves. Branded, Buzan-style mind maps have a thin and mixed evidence base: one small study with a motivation caveat, one null result, and one recent meta-analysis whose effect size could not be retrieved. Most mind-map studies compare against weak baselines such as ordinary note-taking or lecture.

The two kinds of map differ. A concept map has labelled links that state a relation between two ideas (for example "causes" or "is part of"), and it is usually hierarchical. A Buzan-style mind map is radial, uses single keywords on branches, and adds colour and images. The strongest numbers come from concept maps. They transfer to mind maps only to the extent that both share the act of constructing a structured diagram.

Figures that could not be traced and should not be used

  • A claim that mind maps raised children's word recall by up to 32% over lists traces to a vendor blog that cites Toi (2009). The primary source could not be verified. Vendor blog Weak
  • A Substack post quotes an effect size of about g = 1.5 for mind mapping and cites no primary source. It should be ignored. Substack post Folklore
  • A "Liu 2014" meta-analysis on mind maps could not be located, so its effect size is unverified and it should not be cited.
  • No source supports a "10%" improvement from mind maps. The nearest figure is Farrand's motivation-adjusted 15%, which is not a raw difference.
Implication for Radiant

Do not specify or market the app with a claim such as "mind maps improve memory by X%". The defensible claims are that constructing a map yourself helps, that studying someone else's map helps moderately, and that pairing maps with recall practice helps more. A delightful tool might also add real benefit by raising engagement, since Farrand's mind-map group reported lower motivation, but that idea is an inference and has not been tested.

Making versus viewing

Constructing a map beats studying a provided one: g = 0.72 against 0.43 in the 2018 meta-analysis Schroeder 2018 Strong. This matters most for an AI-generated map, because the AI would otherwise do the constructing and leave the learner in the weaker "studying" condition. No controlled study of AI-generated maps and learning was found.

Related generative-learning evidence

The same pattern appears for other learner-made study aids. Figures in this list come from secondary citations and search summaries.

  • Drawing beat controls in 26 of 28 studies, with a median effect of d = 0.40, and summarising had a median of d = 0.50 (Fiorella and Mayer, 2015, as cited in a 2024 paper). Frontiers 2024 Moderate
  • Learner-generated outlines gave g = 0.59 for memory and 0.59 for comprehension. Learner-generated graphic organisers gave g = 0.59 for memory and 0.63 for comprehension. Highlighting gave 0.36 and 0.20. These come from a search summary of work by Ponce, Mayer and Méndez. Springer 2025 Moderate
  • A 2025 meta-analysis of 14 studies (16 comparisons) found that drawing beats reading alone, but that added drawing support such as prompts and scaffolds was not reliably related to outcomes. Springer 2025 drawing Moderate

Generation can also overload

Chang, Sung and Chen (2002) compared map correction (a map about 40% incomplete), scaffold fading and full map generation. They argued that building maps promotes deeper processing but can overload learners, and proposed faded scaffolds as the compromise. Chang 2002 Weak No effect sizes were retrieved.

The evidence on self-generated graphic organisers conflicts. A study by Colliot and Jamet found that an author-provided organiser helped, while generating one versus viewing a ready-made one reduced retention of the covered material. Bohrium summary The Ponce, Mayer and Méndez meta-analysis above favours learner generation. The notes read the conflict as follows: generation helps when the learner is capable and supported. This is an interpretation, not a tested result. Contested The results of Stull and Mayer (2007), who compared learner-generated and author-provided organisers, could not be retrieved.

Ruiz-Primo and colleagues (2001) found that fill-in-the-nodes and fill-in-the-lines formats are not equivalent, and that constructing a map from scratch better captured differences in learners' knowledge structure. This was an assessment study, not a learning-gain study. Ruiz-Primo 2001 A 2026 conference abstract reported that restating expert maps in the learner's own words gave better transfer than filling in blanks or building from a concept list. It is preliminary. AERA abstract Weak

Retrieval-based mapping

Blunt and Karpicke (2014) had students retrieve from a science text by writing a paragraph or drawing a concept map, with the text present or absent. Mapping from memory and paragraph writing were equally effective as retrieval activities, and mapping with the text present was not especially effective. Blunt 2014 Learning Scientists Ortega-Tudela, Lechuga and Gómez-Ariza (2019) conceptually replicated the core result. Ortega-Tudela 2019 Moderate The practical reading is that a map is a good format for retrieval but no better than prose, so the retrieval should be the feature and the map the container.

Unguarded AI help

No controlled study of AI-generated mind maps was found. The nearest evidence suggests that AI help raises performance while the help is available, and that unguarded help can hurt later independent performance.

  • Bastani and colleagues (PNAS, 2025) ran a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high-school maths students. Practice performance rose 48% with a plain GPT assistant and 127% with a guarded tutor. After access was removed, the plain-assistant group did worse than controls (one preprint version gives 17% lower), while the guarded tutor largely removed the harm. One school, maths only. PNAS record Wharton PsyPost Moderate
  • A randomised study of 211 Middlebury undergraduates writing essays found the AI group scored 6.7 percentage points higher on the immediate test and 5.1 points higher a week later. It was text-based, not images, and a preprint. IZA 18792 Weak

These two results point in different directions, and neither concerns maps. The notes treat the guardrail result as the closest guide: let the AI produce material, then make the learner do the effortful step of editing, rebuilding or being quizzed. No study of cognitive offloading with AI-generated maps was found.

Idea for Radiant

Treat the AI output as a draft skeleton for the learner to complete, not a finished study artefact. Offer a study mode that blanks 30 to 40% of nodes or links (the proportion Chang's incomplete-map format used) and fades the scaffold over repeated reviews. Add "explain this branch in your own words" prompts on AI-provided branches. This is a proposed design built on the evidence above, not a tested one.

Retrieval and spacing

Retrieval practice and spaced practice are the two best-supported study techniques in the notes, and map-building alone is weaker than either.

  • Karpicke and Blunt (2011, Science), about 200 students across two experiments: retrieval practice produced more learning than elaborative concept mapping, including on a criterion test that required building a concept map. One secondary summary reports about 50% better after one week, which was not verified in the paper. Students predicted that mapping would work better. The authors note that mapping could itself be used as a retrieval method. Karpicke 2011 Stafforini listing Strong for retrieval practice in general, which is widely replicated; the notes cite this one study for it.
  • Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated practice testing and distributed practice as high utility. Elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaving were moderate. Summarisation, highlighting, the keyword mnemonic, imagery for text and rereading were low. Dunlosky 2013 Strong (a review).
  • Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted and Pashler (2008), over 1,350 participants: the best gap between study sessions is a proportion of the time until the test, about 20% for tests a few weeks away, falling to about 5% for a one-year delay. Secondary examples are 1 day for a one-week test, 11 days for one month, and about 3 weeks for 70 days. Cepeda 2008 York summary Strong for spacing itself. The exact schedule depends on population and material.

The Dunlosky ratings carry a warning for a map app. Rereading a finished map feels productive and sits in the low-utility group. The practical rule is to make recall, not rereading, the default way to revisit a map. Follow-up debate papers on the Karpicke and Blunt result (Lechuga 2015, Blunt and Karpicke 2014 beyond the study above) were not retrieved.

Pictures: when they help

Recognition memory for pictures is very large. Picture benefits in learning depend on what the picture does and on how and when memory is tested. A picture added for decoration does not help and may harm.

Picture superiority and its limits

  • Standing (1973) showed participants up to 10,000 pictures and found near-limitless recognition capacity. An accuracy of about 83% at 10,000 pictures comes from secondary sources. This shows recognition, not that pictures help recall of verbal knowledge. Standing 1973 Strong for recognition.
  • Dual coding theory (Paivio) holds that verbal and imagery systems are separate but linked, so a picture plus a word gives two routes to the memory. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that it "has by no means gained universal acceptance". Critics argue that meaning is stored as propositions rather than images, and that Paivio miscounted the codes. No concreteness-effect numbers were retrieved. SEP dual coding Contested as an explanation; Moderate as a useful frame.
  • Boundary conditions: Weldon and Roediger (1987) found the effect can be eliminated or reversed by changing the encoding task, and it is relatively insensitive to retrieval demands. The effect can vanish with conceptual encoding and conceptual tests, and when the pictures are very similar to each other. Stenberg (2003) reasoned that it would not survive a switch where only meaning carries over, such as studying a picture of a horse and testing with the word "horse". Wikipedia summary Reversal study Moderate The boundary conditions come mostly from a Wikipedia summary of primary papers.
  • Older and impaired adults: a Wikipedia-derived page says the recognition effect grows with age and that Ally, Gold and Budson (2009) found it in mild Alzheimer's disease. Neither claim was checked in a primary paper. Weak

Functions of pictures

Carney and Levin (2002) reviewed 1970s and 1980s research and concluded that carefully constructed text illustrations generally improve performance on text-dependent outcomes. They sort pictures into five functions: representational (depicts what the text says), organisational (gives structure), interpretational (clarifies a hard concept), transformational (recodes hard-to-remember content into a memorable image) and decorative. Carney and Levin BearWorks A secondary summary of the underlying meta-analysis (Levin, Anglin and Carney, 1987) says illustrations other than decorative ones consistently produced greater text learning, and that effects were much larger for adjunct pictures than for figural displays such as diagrams and maps. e-teaching.org JALT summary Moderate for the ranking. The per-category effect sizes were not obtained, so no figure for "decorative" or "transformational" should be quoted.

A separate meta-analysis reported d = 0.40 for animation against static pictures when the animation was representational rather than decorational. Höffler 2007

Mnemonic pictures and the picture-mnemonic products

Mnemonic picture methods give large effects on arbitrary-association learning such as vocabulary, names and facts, mostly in the originators' labs. Dunlosky rates the keyword mnemonic low utility because it only suits keyword-friendly material.

The drawing effect

Drawing a concept beats writing it down, and drawing quality does not seem to matter. Wammes, Meade and Fernandes (2016) ran seven free-recall experiments in which drawn words were recalled better than written ones, often more than twice as many. Wammes 2016 UWSpace Jonker and Wammes (2019) found the effect for both pure and mixed lists. Jonker 2019 Moderate to Strong: many experiments, but from one lab and not pooled in a meta-analysis. The median drawing effect of d = 0.40 across generative studies, given above, is a secondary citation.

AI-generated images

The evidence on AI-generated images is thin and none of it is conclusive.

  • A 2025 preprint of 275 first-year medical students used DALL-E 3 ECG mnemonic images. A non-randomised comparison reported improved retention and higher situational interest. Preprint Weak
  • RetAssist (arXiv, 2024) was a within-subject study of 24 English-as-a-second-language learners comparing generated images with none for vocabulary retelling. The results were not retrieved. RetAssist Weak
  • A critique of a 40-participant study of text against text plus AI image notes it lacked a traditional-image condition, so any advantage cannot be attributed to the AI generation itself. Weak

No study found that AI-generated pictures differ from other pictures in learning. The safe assumption is that they behave like ordinary pictures: recognition benefits but no generation benefit, because the learner did not draw them.

Decoration, emotion and load

Irrelevant additions slightly hurt learning, while visuals that are themselves designed to feel pleasant help a little. The two findings reconcile once the difference is clear: seductive details are interesting but irrelevant extras added on top of the material, whereas emotional design changes the core visuals themselves.

Signalling and colour

Cueing reliably helps retention and somewhat helps transfer. Colour works as a cue only when it carries information and is used consistently.

  • Alpizar, Adesope and Wong (2020): 44 effect sizes from 29 studies, N = 2,726, overall d = 0.38. Alpizar 2020 Strong
  • Schneider, Beege, Nebel and Rey (2018): per a secondary summary, 103 studies, N = 12,201, retention g+ = 0.53 and transfer g+ = 0.33. The figures differ across sources and the paper was not read. TUM summary Springer 2020 Strong in direction; the numbers are unverified.
  • Richter, Scheiter and Eitel (2016): 27 studies, 45 comparisons, N = 2,464, r = 0.17 (95% CI 0.11 to 0.22) on transfer and comprehension, with low-prior-knowledge learners benefiting more. Richter 2016 Bohrium Strong
  • Colour-coding specifics are weak. Skulmowski (2022, as cited in a 2024 review) reported that colour coding helped when used in both learning and retrieval but hurt when used in only one phase. In a physics class survey only about 16% of responses recognised and valued the colour coding unless it was explained. Hall and Sidio-Hall (1994) found knowledge-map study recalled more than text, with self colour-coding interacting marginally (p = .08) with test anxiety. Springer 2024 arXiv Hall 1994 Weak
  • A general review of colour in attention, arousal and memory (Dzulkifli and Mustafar, 2013) found that coloured multimedia gave better attention and memory in earlier work, but the notes saw only a summary of this narrative review. Dzulkifli 2013 Weak to Moderate

No study of colour-blind-safe design was found, so a colour-blind-safe palette is an accessibility recommendation and not an evidence-based one.

Cognitive load

Richly illustrated maps risk extraneous load, which is mental effort spent on processing that does not help learning, such as splitting attention between a legend and a picture or reading the same information three times. Mayer's principles give medians for the relevant effects, from short laboratory lessons, mostly with learners who know little about the subject, and often from the author's own lab. They are direction, not forecasts.

  • Mayer (2017) abstract medians, as quoted by a search summary: coherence 0.70, signalling 0.46, redundancy 0.87, spatial contiguity 0.79, segmenting 0.70, pre-training 0.46, and multimedia d = 1.67 from only five comparisons. A later Mayer table gives coherence 0.86 (18 of 19 tests positive) and segmenting 0.67 (7 of 7), so the numbers move between editions. Mayer 2017 JCAL listing Mayer 2020 Moderate
  • Expertise reversal: methods that help novices lose value or reverse as knowledge grows (Kalyuga, 2007). Chen, Kalyuga and Sweller (2017) argue it is a variant of the broader element-interactivity effect. A 2025 meta-analysis (Tetzlaff and colleagues) reportedly covers 176 effect sizes from 60 experiments (N = 5,924), but only a secondary summary was seen and no pooled value. Kalyuga 2007 Chen 2017 Wikipedia pointer Moderate, with the 2025 numbers unverified.

Split-attention and redundancy meta-analytic numbers (Chen and colleagues, 2018) were not found, so Mayer's redundancy and spatial-contiguity medians are the closest figures.

Emotion and humour

Arousal can enhance memory for the emotional item itself, and the benefit is focal. Kensinger found arousal boosts memory for central details, and that valenced but non-arousing items are enhanced by different, more controlled processes. Kensinger 2004 Kensinger 2009 A 2013 short review says emotion does not always help, and that arousal helps up to a point and then hurts. JINS review Moderate Nothing on the humour effect (Schmidt, 1994) was retrieved, so whether funny or exaggerated node images are supported is unknown from these notes.

Disfluency and aesthetics

Making text harder to read to improve memory has failed to replicate. Diemand-Yauman and colleagues (2011) claimed hard fonts help. Three close replications by Rummer and colleagues (2016) failed, as did Eitel and colleagues (2014) and others. The Xie and colleagues (2018) meta-analysis of 39 experiments (25 articles, N = 3,135) found d = 0.01. Weissgerber and colleagues (2021) questioned that meta-analysis's coding, and a 2023 preregistered replication (N = 96) found no effect. The original authors suggested distinctiveness as the alternative explanation. Weissgerber 2021 Springer 2023 PMC Strong that the effect is absent or negligible.

Beautiful interfaces are rated as more usable, which is a perception effect. Kurosu and Kashimura (1995) had 252 participants rate 26 cash-machine interface variants and found apparent usability correlated strongly with beauty, even though participants had not used the products. Tractinsky replicated it in Israel, and Tractinsky and colleagues (2000) found it after use. A later boundary-conditions study found no effect of aesthetics on usability ratings, and a review says causation is not firmly established. UXPA article Tractinsky Monk and Lelos Moderate for perception, Weak for performance. The processing-fluency literature (Reber, Schwarz and Winkielman, 2004) was not retrieved.

Implication for Radiant

Beauty is justified for adoption and engagement, not as a memory mechanism. Do not use deliberately hard-to-read fonts or styles as a memory aid. Readable type and good contrast are the safe default.

Distinctiveness and space

Distinctiveness helps only relative to its context. A map where every branch is unique in the same way is no more distinctive than a uniform one, and the benefit comes from contrast.

Isolation and bizarreness

McDaniel and Einstein (1986) found that bizarre imagery aided recall when bizarre and common items were mixed within a list, but not when they were varied between lists. McDaniel and colleagues (1995) found that the mixed-list structure drives the effect, and rival accounts based on order encoding exist. The von Restorff isolation effect is within-subject by definition, because the isolated item must differ from the others in the same list. McDaniel 1986 CORE paper Concept summary Moderate, replicable under mixed-list conditions. Bizarre images may also reduce memory for order, so ordered content needs sequence cues such as numbers or arrows (an inference).

Spatial memory and stable layouts

Method-of-loci training, which ties items to places in an imagined route, produces large word-list gains in healthy adults. Dresler and colleagues (2017) compared 23 memory athletes with 23 matched controls and randomised 51 mnemonic-naive men to six weeks of loci training, working-memory training or nothing. Word-list recall rose well beyond the starting 26 to 30 words in the loci group, gains persisted at four months, and brain connectivity moved toward the athletes' pattern. All participants were men, from a single lab, using word lists. Stanford release ScienceDaily Moderate No direct experiment showing that stable node positions on a map improve recall was found, so the layout-stability recommendation is inferred.

The human-computer interaction literature supports stable spatial layouts for documents and icons.

  • Scarr, Cockburn and Gutwin argue that interfaces should support the development of spatial memory and exploit it. Robertson and colleagues' Data Mountain found reliable advantages over browser favourites, and a later paper summarises that spatial layouts supported faster retrieval even after three months. Scarr 2013 Data Mountain Graphics Interface 2020 Moderate for documents and icons, Weak for mind-map nodes, which is an extrapolation.
  • Animation: Bederson and Boltman (1999) found that animating a viewpoint change improved users' ability to reconstruct the information space with no cost in task time. Heer and Robertson (2007) ran two controlled experiments showing that animated transitions between statistical graphics significantly improve graphical perception, meaning object tracking and value estimation. Bederson 1999 Heer 2007 Heer PDF Moderate The "staging" wording attributed to Heer and Robertson could not be verified.
  • Zoomable and tree interfaces: Cockburn, Karlson and Bederson (2008) classify interfaces as overview plus detail (spatial separation), zooming (temporal separation), focus plus context (integrated) and cue-based; only the abstract was retrieved. Cockburn 2008 SpaceTree (Plaisant, Grosjean and Bederson, 2002) uses dynamic rescaling, optimised camera motion and preview icons for unexpanded branches, and a controlled experiment found benefits for returning to previously visited nodes and estimating tree topology. SpaceTree HCIL Moderate Card and Nation (2002), Furnas's fisheye views and Pad++ were not retrieved, so there is no sourced evidence here for overview-plus-detail comprehension benefits.

Chunking

Cowan (2001) argued that working-memory capacity is about four chunks, not Miller's seven plus or minus two, tied to the focus of attention. The view is not universally accepted. Cowan 2001 Moderate It loosely supports keeping the number of visible children per node small. The default of roughly three to five main branches and three to five children per node is a heuristic derived from Cowan, with no direct test on maps. Collapsing and progressive disclosure fit the segmenting principle, but the primary source for segmenting was not retrieved.

What this means for Radiant

The evidence supports a clear order of priorities. Features that make the learner act on the map come first. Features that make the map more beautiful come second, and are justified by engagement and by the limited evidence for emotional design, not by a memory claim.

Editable skeleton, not a finished map

Making versus viewing Strong

The AI should generate a draft structure that the learner edits, reorders and relabels. A study mode that blanks 30 to 40% of nodes or links, with the blanks fading as mastery grows, follows Chang's incomplete-map format. Proposed design

Collapse and recall

Retrieval practice Strong

Hide a branch's children or labels, ask for free recall first, then reveal and record correctness. Make recall the default way to revisit a finished map, and say that rereading feels productive but is a low-utility technique.

Hidden labels and map from memory

Retrieval-based mapping Moderate

Let the learner close the source and rebuild the map from memory. Mapping with the source open was not especially effective.

Spaced review scheduling

Spacing Strong

Schedule reviews per branch. Set the gap from the learner's target date at roughly 10 to 20% of the interval, and use a simple expanding schedule as the default. The exact schedule depends on material.

Meaningful pictures, not decoration

Picture functions Moderate

Each node image should depict the concept. The image prompt should say to depict the concept itself with no background clutter and no unrelated ornament. Keep the text label touching the image, and do not repeat the same information in label, caption and image.

Distinct images among siblings

Picture similarity Weak to Moderate

Give sibling nodes concept-specific images in one coherent style family. Near-identical pictures may lose picture superiority. This is an inference to test.

Richest treatment for key nodes

Isolation effect Moderate

Use a restrained base style and reserve size, colour or an unusual image for the few nodes the learner must remember, such as key facts and exceptions. Keep numbers or arrows on ordered content.

Mnemonic mode for abstract items

Keyword mnemonics Moderate

Choose the picture function per node: a representational picture for a concrete object, a transformational mnemonic picture for a name, date or abstract term, and no decorative filler.

Decoration-off and lean modes

Seductive details and expertise reversal Moderate

A per-user toggle for decoration, images and colour. Novices get the full scaffold, experts a lean view with images collapsed, defaulting to lean for material marked as known.

Stable layout, animated changes

Spatial memory Moderate for documents

Store fixed coordinates, move only new nodes, allow pinning, never re-layout a map the learner has seen, and keep geometry fixed when a subtree is re-rendered as raster or SVG. Animate expand, collapse and re-layout briefly. Add a minimap and branch previews such as child counts or icons.

Sketch input

Drawing effect Moderate to Strong

Add a quick pen or finger doodle on a node, and encourage doodling first with AI polish afterwards, so some generation happens. Drawing quality does not matter for memory, so polish serves beauty and motivation.

Honest marketing

Mind-map claims Contested

Claim that building maps and recalling from them helps. Do not claim a percentage gain for mind maps, for AI-generated pictures, or for picture-mnemonic styles. Position the app as a thinking and structuring tool and make no creativity promises.

Decision

Pair every map-building feature with a retrieval or spacing feature, because mapping alone is not the high-utility technique. Treat AI-generated images as ordinary pictures. Require the learner to choose, edit or replace them, so that some generation takes place. See the picture and SVG subtree page and the AI engine page for how these constraints meet the signature feature.

Other consequences, each following from the sections above. Curved, organic branches are a justified style option on preference grounds, and the curves must never obscure which node a branch connects. Colour should encode category as a hue family, be explained once, and be backed by a second cue such as an icon, position or line style, in a palette safe for colour-blind users. Readable type and strong contrast are the default. For a 3 to 5 branch heuristic, default AI generation to roughly three to five main branches and three to five children per node, with deeper detail collapsed. Labels should be terse, 2 to 5 words, without enforcing a single word.

Limits of this research

Nearly everything here comes from abstracts, press releases, secondary summaries and search snippets. Publishers blocked full-text retrieval for several key papers, and one page fetch returned nothing. Check every number against the primary paper before quoting it in a requirement, a feature description or marketing.

Not verified

The following could not be obtained: the pooled effect size and confidence interval for Shi and colleagues (2023); the existence of the "Liu 2014" meta-analysis; the results of Wickramasinghe (2007); D'Antoni (2010) sample size and statistics; per-category picture-function effect sizes from Levin, Anglin and Carney (1987); the pooled g of Sundararajan and Adesope (2020); Chen and colleagues (2018) split-attention numbers; Schmidt (1994) on humour; Reber, Schwarz and Winkielman (2004) on fluency; the Mintzes comment and O'Day and Karpicke (2021) on retrieval-based mapping; Card and Nation (2002), Furnas fisheye views and Pad++; Paivio's concreteness-effect numbers; the results of Stull and Mayer (2007); and Chang and colleagues' (2002) effect sizes.

  • Several figures came from secondary sources and are marked as such in the text: the two Nesbit and Adesope effect sizes, the 50% retrieval gain in Karpicke and Blunt, the 83% accuracy in Standing, Mayer's medians, the Schneider and colleagues figures and the 2025 expertise-reversal meta-analysis.
  • Sources disagree on some counts: Sundararajan and Adesope's number of studies (58 or 68), and Mayer's coherence and segmenting medians between 2017 and 2020.
  • No controlled study of AI-generated mind maps, of AI-generated node images against drawn or stock images, or of cognitive offloading with AI-generated maps was found.
  • Spatial-memory benefits are shown for documents and icons, not for mind-map nodes. Applying them to nodes is an extrapolation.
  • Colour-blind-safe design had no supporting study, and no systematic list of null studies was compiled.
