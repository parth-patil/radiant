Buzan's laws and how to apply them

This page sets out Tony Buzan's rules for making mind maps memorable and eye-catching, as close to his own words as the research allows, with his reason for each and a clear mark showing whether it was read in his books or only in someone else's summary. It then turns every rule into something Radiant can do. The style presets, the automatic checks and the instructions to the map-generating AI will be built from this page.

Evidence

Two Buzan books were read as text: Use Your Head (Guild Publishing edition, 1984, copyright 1974, 1982, 1984, with printed page numbers) and Mind Map Mastery (Watkins, 2018). The 2018 copy has no usable printed page numbers and its Chapter 1 is missing from the extracted text, so page references for it come from Buzan's own internal cross-references. The Mind Map Book (1993/1996) could not be read, because its archive.org text is borrow-only, so every rule attributed to it here is secondary. Where this page quotes Buzan, the quotation is verbatim from the book text read. Neither book is available online, so book citations are given in words without a link.

Radiant thinking

Radiant thinking is Buzan's name for the way the brain works when it associates ideas outward from a centre, and it is the idea the mind map is meant to mirror. The definition proper sits in Chapter 1 of Mind Map Mastery (referred to elsewhere in the book as "page 33"), which is absent from the extracted text. It therefore cannot be quoted here. What can be quoted shows how he uses the term.

“every human – you and me included – is fluent in the twin faculties of association and imagination from birth”

Tony Buzan, Mind Map Mastery, 2018, ch. 2, "A Creativity Workout" box

“a Mind Map relies on the power of Radiant Thinking rather than on deduction and reduction. As an open-minded thinking tool, a Mind Map charts the processes of the brain in action instead of fixating on conclusions and outcomes.”

Tony Buzan, Mind Map Mastery, 2018, ch. 2, "A Mind Map for All Ages"

“By encouraging radiant thinking, we can make the best use of our creative abilities in an easy, natural way”

Tony Buzan, interview with Stuart Crainer and Des Dearlove, management-issues.com, 4 December 2007 Interview 2007

In the same 2007 interview he adds: "The mind really works in multiple thoughts and directions at the same time". In Chapter 2 of the 2018 book the same pair appears as "the twin forces of imagination and association", and he calls association and imagination the "twin faculties" of an innate "Human Language". He also describes mind maps as radiating "like a starburst" in contrast with trees that grow upward (ch. 3), and links thinking to neurons "with tentacles radiating out from the cell's centre" (Preface).

The sentence often quoted as his definition, that radiant thinking "is intended to reflect the structure and processes of our brains", comes from Adam Tornhill's review of The Mind Map Book, not from Buzan. Tornhill The term "Radiant Thinking" does not occur in the 1984 Use Your Head at all.

Implication for Radiant

Imagination and association are the two pillars Buzan names. Radiant can express imagination through generated artwork, absurd or humorous images and dimension, and association through arrows, codes, cross-links and colour codes. The strongest statement of the idea is the 2018 contrast with "deduction and reduction": a mind map records the thinking, not a conclusion.

The ten laws (2018)

The only complete rule list read in primary text is the set of ten Laws in Mind Map Mastery, Chapter 2, "The Laws of Mind Mapping" (book p. 60). Buzan frames them as a structure for thinking: the Laws "are designed to support your thinking with a structure, allowing it to flourish rather than to get tangled up and flounder." He warns: "If you deviate from them, you won't create a true Mind Map." Following them "frees you up to develop your own unique 'fingerprint', or rather 'eyeprint', style". By Mind Map number 100 he says you will have "attained a highly skilled level", and in Chapter 4 he repeats that the Laws "are a manifestation of the more general laws of good thinking". Law 6 as quoted below is one sentence in the book that covers both the radiating and the taper; the consolidated table later splits it into two rows for product purposes.

Source: Mind Map Mastery, 2018, ch. 2 (local PDF, no printed page numbers; Law list at book p. 60 per Buzan's own cross-reference).

Chapter 2 supplies further detail that is not part of the ten Laws but follows from them.

  • Basic Ordering Ideas. The main branches are "known as Basic Ordering Ideas (BOIs) and constitute the core framework". Prompts include "the most important seven categories", chapter headings, and "What? Where? Who? How? Which? When?". Suggested lenses are structure, function, properties, processes, evaluation, definitions, classification, history and personalities.
  • Hierarchy. "The most general (inclusive) concepts tend to sit closer to the central image". If an outer idea proves more important, "simply thicken up the outer branches where necessary".
  • Upright. "Keep your Mind Map as upright as you can, with its branches as close to the horizontal as possible, which will make the Mind Map much easier to read and recall."
  • Size. "The larger an item, the greater its visual impact and the more likely you will be to recall it." Important words go in "THREE DIMENSIONS".
  • Senses and movement. "well-organized synaesthesia can actually increase the powers of the brain". He asks for "all five senses in your words and images" and for "movement ... through the organic form of the branches".
  • Codes. Simple (crosses, ticks, underlines, circles, triangles, squares) or "elaborate symbols – even shown in 3D", which "reinforce the categorization and hierarchy".
  • Boundaries. Colourful boundaries, "a wavy line or a cloud-like bubble", relate to Miller's 1956 work on chunking. "In Mind Mapping, the outline of a boundary creates a unique shape of its own, making it memorable in its own right". Caution: "If a branch is outlined with a boundary too soon, the Mind Mapper will potentially place themselves in a restrictive prison."
  • Repetition. A word repeating across branches is "not a problem. This is a breakthrough". Underline it, box it at three repeats, use 3-D boxes at four or more, and "use the word ... as the new central image in your next Mind Map" (ch. 4).
  • Mini and master maps. "Mini Mind Maps orbit the main Mind Map like moons around a planet. They explore aspects of the main Mind Map in closer detail without cluttering it up." If there is no room, give them "a page of their own" (ch. 4).
  • Colour in the first draft. Black-and-white drafts are allowed, but "colour is a key element in promoting creativity ... if you initially create your Mind Map in black and white, you will be radically limiting your ability to generate new ideas."

His example of a colour code is the Wu Xing: Wood green, Fire orange, Earth brown, Metal silver, Water blue. Colour is also tied to the von Restorff effect in Chapter 1 (p. 40), which is not in the extracted text. Source for all of the above: Mind Map Mastery, 2018, ch. 2 to 4.

Earlier versions of the rules

Use Your Head, 1984

The 1984 edition predates the Laws list and uses lowercase "mind maps". It gives seven advantages over linear notes, with reasons (Use Your Head, 1984 edition, pp. 91 to 92, ch. 4 "Noting", part B). Verified in his text

The seven advantages of a mind map over linear notes, Use Your Head, 1984, pp. 91 to 92 (quoted or abbreviated as the notes give them)
No.Advantage
1“The centre or main idea is more clearly defined.”
2“The relative importance of each idea is clearly indicated. More important ideas will be nearer the centre and less important ideas will be near the edge.”
3“The links between the key concepts will be immediately recognisable because of their proximity and connection.”
4Recall and review “will be both more effective and more rapid”.
5“The nature of the structure allows for the easy addition of new information without messy scratching out or squeezing in”.
6“Each map made will look and be different from each other map. This will aid recall.”
7“the open-ended nature of the map will enable the brain to make new connections far more readily.”

Other verbatim guidance from that edition:

  • Method (p. 91). “Rather than starting from the top and working down in sentences or lists, one should start from the centre or main idea and branch out as dictated by the individual ideas and general form of the central theme.”
  • Keep the mind free (pp. 92 to 93, 95). “Any 'thinking' about where things should go ... will simply slow down the process ... Do not worry about order or organisation as this will in many cases take care of itself.” And “With the map approach each idea is left as a totally open possibility, so that the map grows organically and increasingly, rather than being stifled.”
  • One word per line (p. 92). “Words should be in 'units', i.e. one word per line. This leaves each word more free hooks and gives note-taking more freedom and flexibility”.
  • Printing (p. 92). Capitals because “a printed map gives a more photographic, more immediate, and more comprehensive feed-back. The little extra time that it takes to print is amply made up for in the time saved when reading back.”
  • Structure (p. 92). “The printed words should be on lines, and each line should be connected to other lines. This is to guarantee that the mind map has basic structure.”
  • Devices (p. 109). Arrows, codes (asterisks, exclamation marks, crosses, question marks), geometrical shapes (a square for the main centre, oblongs next, triangles after), artistic three dimension (ideas "will thus 'stand off the page'"), images and colour.
  • Why key words (pp. 82 to 83). In conventional notes "90 per cent of the words are not necessary for recall purposes", the connections between key words "are interrupted by words that separate them", and "We know that memory works by association". Single words matter because "the single key word or phrase will bring back whole ranges of experience and sensation". He says "word or phrase" here, so the strict single-word rule belongs to the map layout on p. 92.
  • Messy notes (p. 114). “The word 'messy' used in this way refers to the look and not to the content ... The notes which look 'neat' are, in informational terms, messy.” Mapped notes “seldom take more than ten minutes to finalise an hour's notes on a fresh sheet of paper”, and this redraw “will fit in perfectly as the first review”.
  • Uses (pp. 112 to 115). Speech and essay planning ("students were able to complete essays in one third of the previous time while receiving higher marks" at Oxford), two-page note-taking with a mapped left page and a linear or graphic right page, and meetings run on a board-based mind map.
  • Brain framing (p. 110). A note-taking technique "designed to satisfy the needs of the whole brain would have to include not only words, numbers, order, sequence, and lines, but also colour, images, dimension, symbols, and visual rhythms etc: in other words Mind Maps", citing Sperry and Ornstein. The judgement on that claim belongs to the science page.

Source: Use Your Head, 1984 edition, pp. 82 to 83, 91 to 93, 95, 109 to 110, 112 to 115.

The Mind Map Book (1993/1996), secondary only

Secondary only The Mind Map Book could not be read directly. Everything in this subsection comes from other people's summaries, and none of it is quoted from Buzan.

Laws of technique

Summary of The Mind Map Book SlideShare

  • Emphasis: always a central image; images throughout; three or more colours per central image; dimension; synaesthesia; varied size of printing, lines and images; organised and appropriate spacing.
  • Association: arrows within and across branches; colour to enhance memory and creativity; codes for instant connections.
  • Clarity: one keyword per line; print all words; keywords on lines; line length equals word length; connect lines; central lines thicker; boundaries embrace the outline of branches; clear images; paper horizontal; printing upright.
  • Personal style: reflect your own network of thought.

Laws of layout

Same summary

  • Hierarchy.
  • Numerical order.

The summary also lists "break mental blocks" and "prepare" recommendations (blank lines, questions, images, review, quick mind-map checks; a positive attitude, "commit to the absurd", a comfortable workspace) and mistakes to avoid: random, monotonous, disassociated or flattened one-level structures that "aren't really Mind Maps". It states that a messy map is not necessarily bad and that a map is a first draft needing revision.

Ten guidelines (Creativity at Work)

Linda Naiman's page Creativity at Work

  • Start in the centre with an image, using at least three colours.
  • Use images, symbols, codes and dimensions throughout.
  • Select key words and print using "upper or lower case letters".
  • Each word or image alone on its own line.
  • Lines connected, starting from the central image, and thinner as they radiate.
  • Lines the same length as the word or image.
  • Multiple colours for visual stimulation and for encoding or grouping.
  • Develop your own personal style.
  • Use emphasis and show associations.
  • Keep it clear using radial hierarchy or outlines.

Only items 7 and 10 give a reason. Item 3 says upper or lower case, which conflicts with Buzan's own capitals rule in 2018.

What is not a mind map

In Mind Map Mastery, Chapter 3, "What Is Not a Mind Map?", Buzan lists five look-alikes and gives his reason for excluding each. Verified in his text

Buzan's five non-mind-maps (Mind Map Mastery, 2018, ch. 3)
DiagramHis reason, in his words
Spider diagram“They often have a highly structured layout, with legs projecting from a central idea. However, unlike Mind Maps, they don't always use colour and very rarely use images. A spider diagram's legs are usually linear and spindly, rather than organic and varying in thickness.” He adds: “My heart sinks whenever I come across ... the mistaken belief that Mind Maps and spider diagrams are the same thing.”
Pyramid diagramCore concept at the top; “the tendency is for the eye to scan the page from top to bottom in a one-directional, rigid, linear manner, rather than ranging freely”.
Concept map“words and phrases ... placed in boxes or circles”, “connected in a downward-branching hierarchical structure”. Labelled arrows can be “a phrase rather than a single word, so loses the impact that a single keyword has”; “colours and images are not a feature”. They “often have a pedagogical function ... to be learned by rote, whereas Mind Maps can be used ... for brainstorming and devising new strategies”.
Fishbone (Ishikawa, 1960s)“linear, monochrome” and about cause and effect.
Sunburst“The charts do not use imagery. While perhaps useful for summarizing data, they can be hard to read and are not a great tool for inspired creative thinking.”

He also states that a mind map's “use of colour, imagery and visual connections is informal, organic – and essential to its proper functioning” and that “there is no formal iconography, nor a rigorous sense in which a Mind Map can be 'right or wrong'.” On logic: “in a Mind Map things only connect to other elements through the power of association, meaning there is a natural, causal link between them.” Mind maps “do not generally relate to steps, systems or processes”. Chapter 4 adds that many people who report trouble “are not in fact working with Mind Maps at all, but are making spider diagrams”.

The nine-question test

Buzan's test of "a true Mind Map", Chapter 3 of Mind Map Mastery, is the most directly usable item in the book. He asks:

“Is there a clear central concept? Is there a clear central image depicting that concept? Does the diagram radiate from the centre? Is there always one word per branch? Are there any images? Is colour used throughout? Is the Mind Map clear? Is the Mind Map natural and organic in appearance? Is the Mind Map highly visually appealing? If the answer to any of these questions is 'no', you are not looking at a true Mind Map.”

Tony Buzan, Mind Map Mastery, 2018, ch. 3

In the same chapter he lists what would go missing if the Laws were lost: no central image, no colour, only straight lines, no images, "more than one word per branch ... how clumsy it would be", and unequal branch and word length. Chapter 4 adds an eight-point troubleshooting checklist: unlined A4 horizontal, central image in three colours, eye-catching, main branches in different colours, one word per branch, healthy numbers of sub-branches, and images throughout. The checklist also asks of a central word whether it is "in 3D or made to look interesting typographically".

Buzan rejects the idea that older diagrams are mind maps. On Porphyry, Leonardo and Newton see the history page.

Memory and review

Buzan treats memory as the purpose behind most of the rules. In Use Your Head (1984, p. 66, end of the "Memory" chapter) memory is "primarily an associative and linking process which depends in large part on key words and key concepts properly imagined". In Mind Map Mastery (ch. 5, Key Application 6) he cites Collins and Quillian (1969), who "found that our semantic memory ... is organized like a library of sorts, with interrelated categories or nodes", and writes that "everybody has their own spider's web of associations: for example, 'bird' is connected to 'flight' is connected to 'sky'." That is why he prefers a map you make yourself, since the associations are personal.

Memorable images

The 1984 book gives an early list of qualities of memorable connecting images (Use Your Head, 1984 edition, p. 65, ch. 3 "Memory"): one or many of “exaggerated ... absurd ... sexual ... vulgar ... sensual ... moving ... coloured ... imaginative ... pure ...”. The notes say the list continues beyond the lines read. Verified in his text

The acronym SMASHIN' SCOPE is Buzan's later formalisation of this idea. Its expansion, as reported by secondary sources, is Synaesthesia or Sensuality, Movement, Association, Sexuality, Humour, Imagination, Numbers, Symbolism, Colour, Order or Sequence, Positive images and Exaggeration, credited to Buzan and Vanda North's The Memory Book. The review schedule

Use Your Head (1984), pp. 57 to 60, chapter 3 "Memory", section "Memory - review", gives the schedule exactly. It begins with the claim that “within 24 hours of a one-hour learning period at least 80 per cent of detailed information is lost.” Then:

“the first review should take place about 10 minutes after a one-hour learning period and should itself take 10 minutes. This will keep the recall high for approximately one day, when the next review should take place, this time for a period of 2 to 4 minutes. After this, recall will probably be retained for approximately a week, when another 2 minute review can be completed followed by a further review after about one month.”

Later reviews are done “without referring to final notes, jot down on a piece of paper everything that can be recalled”, and then checked. “Both notes and jot-downs should be in the form of Mind Maps as explained on pages 112-114.” The stated benefit is a snowball effect.

Study method and working habits

The Buzan Organic Study Method

Chapter 5 of Use Your Head (1984, from p. 117 to p. 148) sets out the Buzan Organic Study Method, abbreviated BOST. It has an Introduction, a Preparation phase (Time, Amount, Knowledge, Questions) and an Application phase (Overview, Preview, Inview, Review, Noting, Continuing review). The order "is by no means essential and can be changed, subtracted from and added to as the study texts warrant." Verified in his text

  • Noting takes two forms: "Notes made on the text itself" and "A growing mind map". Straight marginal lines mark important material and curved or wavy lines mark "unclear or difficult material" (fig. 54).
  • For the mind map: “start with a central image that captures the essence of that which you are studying”. “Ideally the bulk of Mind Map noting should take place during the latter stages of study”. Keep “enormous 'Central' Mind Maps which summarise and overview the main branches” (also p. 148).
  • “In most cases it will be found that not much more than 70 per cent of that initially considered relevant will finally be used”, and he says to “celebrate!” at completion.

Bursts and stages

“Mind Mapping is most effective when undertaken in bursts of about 20 minutes.” He also says “play is a serious business because it activates the imagination” (Mind Map Mastery, 2018, ch. 2). The stages of a mind mapper's development, in the 2018 chapter 2 section "Development of a Mind Mapper", are three:

  1. Acceptance. “set aside any preconceptions you have about your intelligence, your imagination or your artistic skill. Follow the Laws ... until you become completely familiar with them”.
  2. Application. “apply them by creating as many Mind Maps as you can”.
  3. Adaptation. “With time, you will develop a personal Mind Mapping style. Once you have created hundreds of Mind Maps, you will be ready to experiment by adapting the Mind Map form”.

These are verified in his text. The wording "Accept, Apply, Adapt" attributed to the Mind Map Book is secondary. Buzan's own 2018 account of the origin matters here too: he says linear notes in "blue or black ink" are "by default boring, which means your brain will tune out, dial down and eventually go to sleep". That is the verified form of the "boring" claim, and it concerns monochrome notes, not curves.

Contests give a quality yardstick: the World Mind Mapping Championships mark on "20 different criteria, such as the impact of the imagery, the use of humour (for instance, through puns), the attractiveness of the Mind Map and its originality, and whether it obeys the fundamental principles of Mind Mapping" (Mind Map Mastery, 2018, Preface). Dominic O'Brien, who wrote the foreword, says Mind Mapping "helps me stay on track" and calls himself "grateful to Tony for inventing the Mind Map".

Buzan on software and AI

His position, in Chapter 6 of Mind Map Mastery ("The Future of Mind Mapping"), is that digital maps are acceptable if they obey the Laws. Verified in his text

“Unfortunately, many of the other apps and software packages that claim to be Mind Mapping tools break most of the Laws. In these instances, the software often generates concept maps in which, for example, the use of colour is optional and there are many 'central' ideas; or a number of words or phrases are erroneously crammed into boxes and placed along the branches ... branches are not always connected, differentiated or ordered hierarchically.”

Tony Buzan, Mind Map Mastery, 2018, ch. 6

He proposes checking "the matrix of the Mind Mapping Laws" against "the mini matrix of software programs". The further a person deviates from the Laws, "the less effective the resulting diagram will be". He lists the benefits he grants digital maps: they are editable, backed up, "clear graphics ... easy to read", linkable to other multimedia, open to "post-process analysis", "simple to generate using the supplied drawing tools and icons", and easy to share and annotate. He describes iMindMap as software that "reflects the brain's organic nature ... while obeying the Laws of Mind Mapping", and as "used by over one million people".

His personal preference is stated in the memory section of Chapter 5: “This is one reason why I personally prefer hand-drawn Mind Maps to digital versions, useful though those can be”. The reason is that you must "create your own Mind Map rather than rely on one that has been made by somebody else".

On robots and AI

Chapter 6 of the 2018 book says: "we have yet to develop AI that can itself Mind Map by using association and imagination in meaningful ways". He also writes: "Rather than create new robotic laws, I would like to see the development of AI that is capable of consciously mastering the Laws of Mind Mapping", and "I sincerely believe that the ability to make a Mind Map would act as the ultimate proof of intelligence in a robotic being." He also says he has "yet to meet a robot that can create a Mind Map independently from human input" (the notes give only this fragment, not the full sentence). He cites a 2017 Rutgers computational-art study and Gary Marcus in the New York Times (29 July 2017) on the limits of AI. Nothing from 2018 onward on generative AI exists, because he died in 2019.

He also describes 3D maps. Marek Kasperski proposes them, saying a central theme would be "like holding somebody's brain", and Buzan writes: "We are now in the process of refining 3D iMindMap software".

Both lines might exist in Buzan's other books, in particular The Mind Map Book, which could not be read. The claim here is only that they were not found in the two texts read.

Where practitioners disagree

Jamie Nast, writing in "Mind Mapping or Idea Mapping?" (16 May 2009), says the laws have value "when applied appropriately", but that "some people feel their creativity is restricted by them". Clients "nearly abandoned the tool because of this (especially by the one-word-per-line law)". She teaches the laws "in the form of guidelines", and argues "When and where the laws should be applied should be determined by the purpose for which the map is being created." Nast 2009 Buzan's own contrast is his "true Mind Map" test and his statement that deviation means you "won't create a true Mind Map". The two views are both verified in print, and they do not agree.

Three other points narrow the dispute.

  • Phil Chambers, a Buzan associate, treats phrases as a defect. He describes his school-era maps as "little more than spider diagrams with lots of colour but lacking structure and including phrases" (Mind Map Mastery, 2018, ch. 5).
  • Buzan's own 1984 text says "key word or phrase" in the discussion of notes (pp. 82 to 83) while insisting on "one word per line" for the map (p. 92). A production-audio article advises "one word or phrase per branch", seen in a search summary only. Production Expert
  • Marek Kasperski's phrase about making "very small concept maps to large ones" while following the Laws shows the publisher uses the terms loosely. It is a contributor's view that Buzan reprinted, not a rule change. Joyce Wycoff summarised Buzan's guidelines in 1991 as "A central focus or graphic representation of the problem is placed in the center of a page; ideas are allowed to flow freely", cited secondhand. Nancy Margulies's reader reviews say she "breaks some traditional 'Mindmapping' rules", which she calls "Mindscaping". Crown House

The capitals correction

The first research pass found a conflict over upper or lower case, because a secondary list said "upper or lower case letters". The primary text resolves it: Buzan says capitals for keywords (Law 4), and permits lower case only in outer sub-branches to show lower importance (Mind Map Mastery, 2018, ch. 2). The secondary list is not his wording.

A critical review of the Mind Map Book adds that Buzan "rarely references any scientific support for his claims". Tornhill For research testing the rules, see the science page.

The one-word rule is the clearest place where later research and practitioner experience may part from Buzan. Radiant should keep both a strict and a relaxed mode, because Buzan's own Adaptation stage and Nast's guidelines both allow personal departure, while his true-mind-map test does not.

A "Buzan check" feature

Idea for Radiant

This feature has no prior art and is proposed, not established. Radiant could run Buzan's nine questions as an automatic report on any map, and offer it as a quality gate on AI output. It would show each question as pass or fail and name the nodes that fail, for example the branches with more than one word. Because Buzan's 2018 text frames the test as a check on software, the report could be labelled "Buzan check" in the interface, with a note that it follows his criteria and not a scientific standard.

How each of Buzan's nine questions could be tested automatically (proposed design)
QuestionPossible automatic test
Is there a clear central concept?The map has exactly one root node with a non-empty label.
Is there a clear central image?The root has an image or SVG attached.
Does the diagram radiate from the centre?Every branch descends from the root, with a roughly even angular spread.
Is there always one word per branch?Every non-root label is a single token, unless the relaxed preset is on.
Are there any images?The share of nodes with an image or symbol is above a threshold.
Is colour used throughout?Each main branch has a distinct colour and its descendants inherit it.
Is the Mind Map clear?No overlapping labels, and a minimum gap between siblings.
Is it natural and organic in appearance?Branches are curved, with a taper and some variation.
Is it highly visually appealing?No automatic test is credible. This is a human judgement, optionally scored by an AI against the World Mind Mapping Championships criteria of imagery impact, humour, attractiveness and originality.

The tests are the report's proposals, not Buzan's. His nine questions are verbatim above. The AI generator's brief should carry the same laws as a checklist, and concept-map features such as boxed phrases, optional colour and multiple centres are exactly what he calls non-mind-maps, so Radiant should keep them out of its default preset. A relaxed preset is a deliberate and labelled departure. For the generator itself, see the AI engine page.

What this means for Radiant

  • A strict Buzan preset is fully specifiable from rows 1 to 23 of the consolidated table, in his own words.
  • Rows 29 to 33 should be marked as unverified or contested wherever they appear in the product.
  • The one-word-per-branch rule is a thinking-process rule as well as a style rule: choosing one word forces discrimination. An AI that writes whole phrases defeats the stated purpose. The generator should be told to choose the best single word in strict mode.
  • Branch length equal to word length is a layout rule: the branch is the underline of the word, and not a box around it.
  • The nine-question test and the World Mind Mapping Championships criteria give ready-made acceptance and quality rubrics.
  • Buzan wants AI "capable of consciously mastering the Laws" and also preferred hand-drawn maps, so generated maps should be editable and redrawable, and per-map personal style should be supported.
