How to use mind maps well

A mind map earns its keep when you build or rework it yourself, test yourself from it, and come back to it on a schedule. Looks matter mostly through meaning and contrast, and much of the popular visual advice is preference and not memory science. This page combines practitioner advice with what the evidence supports, says where the two disagree, and ties each practice to a feature that Radiant could offer.

Evidence

The practical advice here rests on two bodies of material. Practitioner advice comes from forums, blogs and educators, and is covered in detail on the community voice page. The experimental evidence is presented on the science page, and Buzan's own rules are on the Buzan page. This page does not repeat them and links to them instead.

When a map is the right tool

Practitioners endorse maps for messy, exploratory work and for relationship-heavy study material. David Sparks puts it as outlines for information that already has structure and “mind maps are all about taming chaos.” He mind-maps to find organisational options when starting a book and to learn something new, and uses outlines for contracts, chronologies and structured posts. A workflow that works

The workflow below combines the most repeated practitioner advice with the techniques the science page rates highly. It is a synthesis written for this report, and its order is a proposal, not a published method.

  1. Gather. Collect the source material and write a guiding question.
  2. Draft from memory. Build a first map without looking at the source.
  3. Check. Compare against the source, or ask AI to point out gaps.
  4. Restructure. Reorder, relabel and add labelled links where relations matter.
  5. Illustrate. Add pictures to a few key nodes.
  6. Recall and review. Hide branches and recall them, then repeat on a schedule.

Working with AI-made maps

An AI-generated map saves the slowest step and removes the step that does the learning. The advice that follows treats the AI output as a draft.

  • Treat it as a draft. MemX says AI maps tend to include everything, which overloads the reader, and that construction beats studying someone else's map. MemX
  • Rework it. Editing, reordering and relabelling make the learner do some of the effortful work. The creating-versus-studying gap in the meta-analysis (g=0.72 against 0.43) is the closest evidence. Schroeder 2018
  • Quiz yourself. Hacker News user crucialfelix wants LLM apps that “ask questions, test understanding.” HN 2024
  • Expand selectively. Use AI to expand one branch at a time, after the overview is understood. Progressive expansion rests on segmenting and working-memory evidence rated moderate on the science page.
  • Use it as a roadmap if you are self-taught. knighthack: “A prepared outline/mind-map gives you a roadmap for learning beforehand.” HN 2024
Risk

In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high-school maths students, practice performance rose 48 percent with plain GPT access and 127 percent with a guarded tutor. After access was removed, the plain-GPT group did worse than controls, and the guarded tutor largely removed the harm. It was one school and maths only, and it did not involve mind maps. No controlled study of AI-generated mind maps was found. Bastani 2025

Making maps memorable

The evidence supports a short list. The rules below are ordered from best supported to least. The numbers behind them are on the science page.

  • Meaningful pictures, not decoration. Node images should carry the concept. Decorative extras hurt novices in short lessons, although this is contested. Strength: Moderate.
  • Distinct images among siblings. Picture benefits are lost when the pictures look alike. This is a design inference. Strength: Weak to Moderate.
  • Emphasis by contrast. Distinctive items are recalled better when they are mixed with ordinary ones. Give a few key nodes a different size, colour or style and keep the rest quiet. A map where every branch is equally ornate is no more distinctive than a plain one. Strength: Moderate for bizarreness, Weak for humour, which has no source. The Sung-derived GRINDE framework also has an emphasis idea, but its source is unverified.
  • Stable positions. Spatial layouts support retrieval when they stay stable, and the memory-palace tradition rests on the same idea. The evidence is for documents and icons, not for mind-map nodes. Strength: Moderate for interface documents, Weak for maps.
  • Colour as a code. Colour that is tied to meaning aids attention and learning, and it should be used consistently. Colour used as arbitrary decoration has no support. Strength: Strong for cueing, Weak for the claim that colour is memory-friendly in itself.
  • A few chunks per level. Working memory holds about four chunks, so each node should show a small number of children, with deeper detail collapsed. Strength: Moderate and not tested on maps.
  • Sketch some nodes yourself. Drawn words were recalled better than written ones, and drawing quality did not seem to matter. Strength: Moderate.

Practitioners add one more: practise drawing many small cartoon-like images (Mnemon, Art of Memory 2017). Art of Memory The practitioner and evidence views agree on that point.

Where they disagree: Buzan's one-keyword-per-line rule has no supporting evidence in the sources found, so the app should allow short phrases. Paul Foreman gives the practitioner view that “a phrase can stop the train of thought.” Foreman slides See the Buzan page for his rules.

Making maps look good

All readable visual guidance traces back to Buzan's laws, and the concrete rules agree across Paul Foreman's own walkthrough, IQ Matrix and an Xmind design article. No championship marking rubric was found, so this list reflects practitioner convention. The evidence that looks matter for memory is weak. Curved branches, for example, are supported only as a preference.

Decision

Colour saturation is a real disagreement. Buzan wants vivid colours, Foreman wants light ones, and Xmind wants low saturation. No source tests which is best for memory. The recommendation is to offer palette presets (vivid, light, low-saturation) and apply one consistently across a map, instead of enforcing one rule.

Two cautions. First, Rohde's stance is “Ideas, Not Art”, and sketchnotes are for the note-taker first. A reviewer reports it is “not about making art, but about capturing ideas using our verbal and visual senses simultaneously.” Seah review Second, the science page finds no evidence that polished art helps memory, so beauty should be justified by adoption and motivation and not by a memory claim.

What this suggests for generated node images

These are proposed design rules, not tested ones.

  • A fixed symbol dictionary per map, so the same type of concept always gets the same motif.
  • One setting or palette per main branch, and optionally a recurring mascot.
  • Use images for concrete, namable things, and keep relationship structure in the map itself. Students split work this way: Anki for retention, picture mnemonics as a supplement, and maps for relationships.

Cautions about AI-generated images

A Jisc pilot (May 2025) found that AI-generated images “often included inaccurate or inconsistent elements” (“extra fingers, twisted limbs, impossible shadows”), that “the more they iterated an image, the more distorted it became” (Jisc says this has since improved with a newer image model), that text inside images was weak, and that defaults were “Western-centric, stereotypical imagery.” It also noted, “Sometimes stock images can be just as good.” Jisc 2025

Keeping maps manageable

Size is the most reported practical limit. The remedies users describe are consistent.

  • Split and link. A Hacker News commenter warned that big maps get unwieldy and should be split into linked smaller maps (seen as a snippet). Weak HN snippet
  • Collapse. A request for a way to hide sub-nodes (JepZ) is among the recurring feature wishes. HN 2018
  • Limit branches. Foreman advises main branches of “no more than 11” for memory, and the working-memory evidence points lower, at about four chunks per level.
  • Know the limit of a map. ljw1001 notes outlines fit “a lot more information in a standard screen.” HN 20903273 A medical-student essay says untaught maps can be “overly simplistic” or misrepresent complex relationships (snippet). Weak The Pathologist

Limits of this research

  • Several educators' positions could not be checked in their own words. Justin Sung's views come from third-party and AI summaries, and his own pages could not be obtained. Third-party summaries disagree on what the GRINDE acronym stands for, and its expansion is unverified, so it is not given here.
  • Cal Newport, Thomas Frank, Sunni Brown and Dan Roam were not obtained, and Mike Rohde's book was not read. Genovese, Boukobza, Thum and other admired practitioners could not be retrieved. Practitioner books were not read.
  • No judging rubric was found. A claim of 23 official championship criteria appears only in book sales text, and the rubric tables from a university competition and a 2009 medical-education paper could not be read.
  • Several items above were seen only as search snippets, and the evidence table relies on the science page, whose figures were largely taken from abstracts and summaries. Check primary papers before any number becomes a requirement.
  • Design items rated as inference need user testing, including sibling-image distinctiveness, white space and layout stability for mind maps.
